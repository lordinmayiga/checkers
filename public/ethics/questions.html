  • 2016 Q2A • 4 marks
    What is a professional code of ethics?
    Model Answer & Marker Words

    A professional code of ethics is a written set of principles and canons issued by a professional society that states the standards of conduct expected of its members. The engineering code has three components: the fundamental canons, which articulate the basic components of ethical engineering; the rules of practice, which clarify and specify the canons in detail; and professional obligations, which elaborate the obligations engineers have.

    It is not legally binding. It is meant to be understood and lived by, and it gives the public a clear idea of what to expect from a responsible engineer. Example: the first canon, hold paramount the safety, health, and welfare of the public.

  • 2016 Q2C • 6 marks • 2017 Q3C • 4 marks
    Describe any FOUR limitations of a professional code of ethics. (2017: Discuss two limitations associated with the use/application of professional codes or codes of conduct.)
    Model Answer & Marker Words
    1. General and vague wording. Codes cannot be applied straight away to all situations, and it is impossible to foresee the full range of moral problems in a complex profession like engineering.
    2. Clauses can come into conflict. Codes give no guidance on which clause has priority, creating moral dilemmas. Example: loyalty to an employer against the safety of the public.
    3. Not the final moral authority. Taking the code as the last word is ethical conventionalism, accepting one set of conventions as right.
    4. Separate codes make ethics seem relative. Oldenquist and Slowter noted that different societies' codes can make members feel ethics is more relative than it is and make the public think none is really right.

    For the 2017 two-limitation version, give limitations 1 and 2 in full. Close with: the codes are not perfect but steps in the right direction.

  • CAT 2021-22 Q1D
    Discuss four limitations of the use of professional codes of conduct and use one of the ethical theories either to support or refute these limitations.
    Model Answer & Marker Words

    Limitation 1: general and vague wording. Codes cannot be applied straight away to every situation. It is impossible to foresee the full range of moral problems in a complex profession like engineering.

    Limitation 2: clauses come into conflict. Codes give no guidance on which clause has priority, which creates moral dilemmas, for example loyalty to an employer against the safety of the public.

    Limitation 3: not the final moral authority. Taking the code as the last word is ethical conventionalism, accepting one set of conventions as right.

    Limitation 4: separate codes make ethics seem relative. Oldenquist and Slowter noted that different societies' codes can make members feel ethics is more relative than it is, and make the public think none is really right.

    Now use one theory (Kantian ethics below, with a utilitarian partial refutation).

    Using Kantian (duty) ethics to support limitations 2 and 3. For Kant, a rule is binding only if it can be willed as a universal law, and duties must not contradict each other. A code with clauses that conflict and gives no priority leaves the engineer without a clear duty, so the limitation holds. Kant also holds that moral authority comes from reason and the categorical imperative, not from the conventions of a society, so treating a code as the final moral authority is ethical conventionalism, which duty ethics rejects.

    Using utilitarianism to refute limitation 1 in part. A utilitarian would say general wording is acceptable if the code, on balance, produces more good than harm: broad canons are applied by judging consequences in each case, so vagueness is a flexibility as well as a weakness.

    Using Kantian ethics to support limitation 4. Separate codes for separate societies make ethics look relative, which Kant rejects: a universal law binds every rational being, so moral rules should not vary from one society or body to another.

    The unofficial solution applies consequentialism to support all four limitations. Either approach is acceptable if you name the theory and link it to a specific limitation. Theory definitions are in t01.

  • QB E3 • 7 marks
    What is the difference between ethics and law?
    Model Answer & Marker Words

    Definitions. Law is a body of rules created to guide conduct, made by the state. Ethics is concerned with rules or guidelines for morals and socially approved conduct.

    1. Purpose. Law creates rules to guide conduct; ethics only offers guidance on conduct.

    2. Values. Law balances competing values and settles them in a rule. Ethics addresses situations in which competing values clash, for example loyalty to an employer against duty to the public.

    3. Enforcement. Law punishes illegal conduct through formal structures such as courts and penalties. Ethics has incentives and disincentives created by a group, formal (a professional body) or informal (peer disapproval).

    4. Coverage. The law does not address all ethical dilemmas, so something can be legal but unethical.

    5. Conflict. Where the two conflict, legal standards must give way to ethical standards.

    Example. Owning a slave before the American Civil War was legal but immoral; parking in a no-parking zone to help an injured person is illegal but moral.

  • 2017 Q3D • 6 marks (also 2021-22 exam Q2B)
    Explain the difference between moral and legal responsibility (mention two points of difference). Illustrate your explanation with an example where the two differ.
    Model Answer & Marker Words

    Moral responsibility is the obligation to act according to ethical principles, based on conscience, personal values and socially approved conduct. Legal responsibility is the obligation to obey rules made by the state, defined as duties, rights and allowable conduct.

    Point 1: enforcement. Legal responsibility is enforced through formal structures (courts) and breach is punished by penalties or sanctions. Moral responsibility is enforced only by incentives and disincentives from a group, formal or informal, and by conscience.

    Point 2: coverage. The law does not address all ethical dilemmas, so moral responsibility reaches further than legal responsibility. Legal duties may not meet the standard of ethical conduct.

    Example where they differ. Parking in a no-parking zone to help an injured person is illegal but moral. Owning a slave before the American Civil War was legal but immoral. Where they conflict, legal standards must give way to ethical standards.

  • Set 4 slide 6 • case
    You are the manager at Big-Mart. You fired Al, a sales clerk, after he punched a customer. A Mega-Mart manager phones and asks if Al is "good with customers". What do you do? Use the steps for resolving a dilemma.
    Model Answer & Marker Words

    Facts. Al punched a customer and admitted it; I fired him; Mega-Mart is asking for a reference.

    Issues. Honesty against fairness to Al; safety of Mega-Mart's customers against Al's chance of a new job; my firm's legal exposure.

    Primary stakeholders. Al, Mega-Mart and its customers, Big-Mart and me.

    Possible solutions. (a) Say "no comment". (b) Say Al is great with customers. (c) Say truthfully that Al was dismissed after an incident with a customer. (d) Refer the caller to HR for a factual reference.

    Evaluation. Under utilitarian theory (greatest good to the greatest number) truth protects many future customers. Under rights theory Al has a right to fair treatment and Mega-Mart's customers a right to safety. Under the categorical imperative, "what if everyone lied about dismissed staff?" shows option (b) fails. The front page test rules out (b) outright.

    Decision. Option (d), or (c) if policy allows: a factual, documented statement with no opinion. Action. Do it, and record what was said.

  • CAT 2021-22 Q2d (also 2020-21 paper)
    Briefly describe how ethical dilemmas arise in the practice of the Engineering profession.
    Model Answer & Marker Words

    An ethical dilemma arises when a choice must be made between options that implicate competing values, rights and goals, so that either choice brings potential harm to the decision maker or to others. In engineering this happens in several ways.

    1. Safety against cost. A manager or client wants a design that meets the budget and deadline but is not fully safe. Approving it breaks the duty to hold paramount the safety, health and welfare of the public; refusing risks the engineer's job (the bridge case in t07).

    2. Loyalty against duty to the public. The engineer owes loyalty to the employer or client and also a duty to society, and the two clash when the employer wants a flaw hidden.

    3. Truth against obedience. The engineer is asked to say or sign something untrue, as in the nuclear-reactor case where a public statement could be shaped by a government.

    4. Conflict of interest. Personal or financial interests interfere with professional obligations, for example favouring a client over the public (t09).

    5. Competing canons. The code itself can pull two ways at once, for example confidentiality to a client against the public's safety (t11).

    beyond the slides The solution files add: conflicting priorities between deadlines, budget and client demands; environmental impact against development goals; and new technology that the law and codes have not caught up with.

    Each case is a dilemma because the engineer cannot satisfy every value at once and the decision has a ripple effect on others.

  • ethics-cat-1 Q3b • 6 marks
    Briefly describe the steps a given professional would take while attempting to resolve ethical dilemmas.
    Model Answer & Marker Words

    1. Identify relevant facts: establish what has happened and what is known.

    2. Identify relevant issue(s): name the conflict of values, for example safety against cost.

    3. Identify primary stakeholders: those affected, such as the public, client, employer and the professional.

    4. Identify possible solutions: list the realistic options.

    5. Evaluate each possible solution using theories: utilitarian (greatest good to the greatest number), rights, justice, the categorical imperative ("what if everyone took such action?") and the front page test.

    6. Compare and assess consequences for each stakeholder, short and long term.

    7. Decide on solution: choose the best option and justify it.

    8. Take action: implement the decision.

    Jenny recently completed her master's degree and was hired for her dream job with the local county government. In her first year she noticed that grant funds were being mismanaged and misallocated, and that coworkers used county-owned materials, including cars, for personal business. She was most shocked by the hiring practices. Applicants had to take exams proctored by government employees, and the results decided whether they were hired and for what. The proctors were letting applicants cheat because the applicants had already been chosen, many of them friends of current employees. Jenny reported this to Matt, the department's business manager and second-in-command, who said: "You heard nothing, you saw nothing; you say nothing." Jenny felt trapped. She needed the job to pay off graduate-school loans and loved the work. She also feared that leaving her first job in under a year would look bad and harm her future chances in government. But she was very uncomfortable in a culture of corruption. Describe Jenny's ethical dilemma and explain what she should do as a young professional in her circumstances.
    Model Answer & Marker Words

    Jenny's ethical dilemma

    This is an ethical dilemma because a choice must be made between competing values: honesty and integrity, and her duty to the public who fund the county, against her own job security, her loan repayments and her career. Both obvious options are costly. If she stays silent she becomes complicit in corruption. If she reports it she risks retaliation or dismissal. There is potential harm to the decision maker (loss of job, bad record, loans) and potential harm to others (honest applicants denied jobs, taxpayers, the public). The decision has a ripple effect on her future career.

    What she should do (steps)

    Facts. Misallocated grants, personal use of county property, rigged exams. She has told her superior, who ordered silence.

    Issues. Corruption and fraud, unfair hiring, a superior who is part of the cover-up, her own livelihood.

    Stakeholders. Jenny, honest applicants, the department head, Matt, the county and taxpayers.

    Options. (a) Stay silent. (b) Resign quietly. (c) Document the evidence and report it above Matt to the department head or the county's audit or anti-corruption body. (d) Report outside (for example to the Inspectorate of Government) if internal channels fail.

    Evaluation. Option (a) fails the categorical imperative ("what if everyone kept quiet about corruption?"), the justice test (honest applicants lose) and the front page test. It does the greatest harm to the greatest number under the utilitarian view. Option (c) protects public rights and justice, and also her integrity.

    Decision and action. Jenny should take action by keeping dated notes and copies of evidence, reporting first to a higher authority in the department than Matt, and escalating to an external body if nothing is done, using the proper channels and not leaks to the press. She should also seek advice from a trusted mentor or lawyer and check what whistleblower protection exists. Her duty to the public welfare and to honesty outweighs the temporary inconvenience to her career. See whistleblowing in t21.

  • QB Sec B Q2b • 14 marks
    If engineers are not afforded the luxury of seeking perfection, it follows that they must be willing to risk failure. Do you agree? Discuss this in view of engineering product safety and ethics. [reconstructed from garbled scan]
    Model Answer & Marker Words

    Position. I agree that engineers must accept that absolute safety is never possible, but this does not allow carelessness. Engineers must keep the risk acceptable, informed and disclosed, and hold public safety paramount.

    For the statement.

    1. True safety never exists; risk is reality, safety is fantasy. Safety means acceptable risk, so some risk always remains.
    2. Safety has a price. Total cost T = P + S has a minimum at point M. Pushing risk to zero makes primary cost P unaffordable, so a product nobody can buy helps nobody.
    3. Design is uncertain: standard data are statistical averages, components have tolerance, and new applications and environments appear.
    4. Testing cannot be trusted uncritically: a prototype may not represent the population and destructive tests cannot be run on every unit.
    5. Engineering is social experimentation: every new product is tried out on society, so some failure is possible.

    Against, or the limits.

    1. Willingness to risk failure is not willingness to hide it. Risk must be disclosed so that those exposed give informed consent. A voluntary risk is tolerated far more than an imposed one.
    2. Failure also has secondary costs: returns and warranty expenses, lost goodwill, litigation, lost customers and rework. Cutting safety to save money is false economy.
    3. Engineers must learn from recorded failures (Stephenson), test, simulate and use quality assurance to keep risk low.

    MEMORANDUM. To: the Judge. From: Law Clerk. Re: Mofonic Games and Bunten v. Schaeffer.

    Summary. Mofonic has a strong copyright claim over the game discs Schaeffer copied and sold, a likely trademark claim, and a weaker claim over the free video. The recommended remedy is an injunction, destruction of the discs and an award of Schaeffer's profit.

    Mofonic: copyright infringement. Stormcrasher is an original work (software, graphics, story, characters, sound), and Mofonic owns the copyright as the creator that paid for and directed it. Copyright gives the owner five rights, and Schaeffer exercised all five without permission. The four bullets below cover them, with public display and public performance taken together.

    • Reproduction. He copied whole game discs 300 times, and copied clips from many scenes to build the video.
    • Distribution. He mailed 300 copies to buyers.
    • Production of derivative works. The new ending changes the game's story and scenes. A new version of the work, fixed on a disc, is a derivative work.
    • Public display and public performance. He posted the video on popular fan sites, showing the clips publicly.

    Mofonic's sales were not lost, since buyers already owned the game, but infringement does not require a lost sale.

    Bunten: copyright. This claim is weak. She spoke lines in a studio Mofonic rented, directed by Mofonic's producers, for a flat fee and a bonus. A court will probably treat her performance as a work made for hire owned by Mofonic, or at least as licensed to Mofonic. The contract is silent, so she could argue she is a joint author of the voice performance, but courts are reluctant to give one performer separate copyright in her part of a finished audiovisual work. Many legal systems recognise a right of publicity (protection against commercial use of a person's identity without consent), and false endorsement (suggesting she approved the product). Her resemblance and voice are her own, and her contract with Mofonic does not license Schaeffer. She may also object that her words were re-arranged to say things she never said.

    B. Defences (5 marks)

    • Purpose and character of use. For him: a fan wanting to give an alternative ending, a creative, transformative and critical purpose; the video was free. Against him: the discs were sold for $10, a commercial purpose that earned about $800 profit; and it is not educational.
    • Nature of the work. Against him: a game and its story are creative, imaginative works, which are less permissible than non-fiction.
    • Amount used. For him: from each scene he took only two or three words, small portions. Against him: on the discs he copied the entire game, and the ending he replaced is the part fans care about.
    • Effect on the market. For him: each buyer already owned the game and paid again for nothing Mofonic sold, so Mofonic lost no sale and he kept the originals. Against him: a market exists for licensed alternate endings and add-ons, and if every fan could copy and sell modified discs the effect would not be negligible.

    Assessment. Fair use is likely to succeed for the free video and likely to fail for the sold discs.

    3. Customisation and ideas. He can say that the player designs Thessia's look in this game, so appearance is the player's own choice, and that ideas such as "Thessia lives" are not protected, only the expression.

    4. Against Bunten. She has no separate copyright (work for hire; she was paid $150,000 or more); the voice taken is tiny; and the work is fan commentary protected as free expression. Against trademark, he can say the edition was plainly a fan product, though the name works against him.

    • Injunction. An order that Schaeffer stop copying and selling the discs and remove the video from the fan sites if it is held to infringe. This is the main remedy and it suits both claimants.
    • Impounding and destruction. beyond the slides The court can order the remaining blank and burned discs destroyed.
    • Damages and profits to Mofonic. beyond the slides Mofonic can recover its actual loss plus any profit of Schaeffer's attributable to the infringement, or statutory damages per work, which can be higher if the infringement was wilful. Actual loss is small because each customer already owned the game; Schaeffer's profit is about $3,000 revenue minus $2,200 costs = $800. The law allows him to deduct costs only if he proves them. Mofonic can also ask for its legal costs.
    • Remedies for Bunten. If her publicity claim succeeds: an injunction and damages equal to the commercial value of her identity, such as the licence fee she would have charged. If she proved joint authorship she could share income, but that would be claimed from Mofonic, not Schaeffer.
    • Limits. If fair use covers the video, no remedy for it. The court should avoid double recovery of the same $800 profit between the two claimants.

  • QB E2 • 6 marks • 2016 Q1d • CAT 2021-22 Q1.4
    Characterize and distinguish between consequentialist ethical theories and deontological ethical theories. Give one example of each.
    Model Answer & Marker Words

    Consequentialist (teleological) theories hold that acting ethically means acting in such a way that the outcome is good. An act is judged only by its consequences; no act is a priori good. Example: utilitarianism, which calls an act right if it increases aggregate happiness.

    Deontological theories hold that acting ethically means respecting one's duties and obligations. Each single act is itself good or bad, regardless of its consequences. Example: Kantianism, where a rule is right if it can be universally applied and treats people as ends.

    The distinction.

    • Focus: consequentialism looks at the outcome; deontology looks at the act and the duty.
    • Lying to save a life: the consequentialist may allow it because the outcome is good. The deontologist forbids it because "do not lie" is a duty.
    • Weakness: consequentialism can sacrifice a minority. Deontology allows no exceptions to rules.

It also treats the future users and the customer as means, not ends, because they are deceived about the engine's reliability. Kantianism puts duty and principle (honesty, integrity) above consequences, so James must do his duty to test thoroughly, report honestly and refuse to issue a report the tests do not support, even if this disobeys Bill.

    (C) Four relevant virtues and the actions they support.

    • Integrity (honesty, adherence to moral principles): James refuses to compromise the reliability of the FANX and does not misreport results.
    • Diligence (careful, persistent effort): he completes all necessary tests meticulously to get accurate reliability data.
    • Prudence (sound judgment and caution): he weighs the risk of rushing and puts long-term safety before the deadline.
    • Professionalism (meeting the highest standards of the profession): he puts quality and thoroughness of testing before pleasing superiors.

    (D) Best framework. Deontological (Kantian) ethics. The problem is a clash between an engineer's duty to ensure safe, reliable products and pressure from a superior and short-term gain. Deontology treats the duty as binding regardless of consequences, so James is supported in disobeying orders and accepting a missed deadline. Utilitarianism reaches the same verdict but relies on predicting future harm, which is uncertain, and virtue ethics describes the person James should be rather than telling him what to do in this dilemma. Whichever theory you choose, justify it.

    Compare and contrast ethical egoism and duty ethics as decision-making frameworks for engineers.
    Model Answer & Marker Words

    Ethical egoism. Individuals ought to act in their own self-interest, with the focus on their long-term best interest. An act is right if it benefits the person who does it. For an engineer, the decision test is "what is best for me and my career?" Its supporters say each individual knows what is in his best interest. Its weakness is that it can lead to selfishness and disregard for others. Some people gain power and abuse it, and a client or the public who depend on the engineer could be harmed.

    Duty ethics (deontology). Actions are right or wrong in themselves, because they follow moral rules or duties, regardless of the consequences. It looks at intention (good will), not outcome. For an engineer the test is "what does my duty require?", for example to protect public safety even if it costs the firm. Its weakness is that it can be rigid, and duties can conflict (see Kantianism in t01).

    Similarity. Both are action-guiding frameworks that tell the engineer what question to ask. (An egoism example, a manager choosing a supplier for a personal commission, is in the next question.)

    Difference. Egoism judges an act by its outcome for oneself (it is teleological: consequences to the agent). Duty ethics judges the act by duty, regardless of consequences. Egoism puts self-interest first and measures an act by what it gains the agent; duty ethics puts duty and moral rules first and may demand sacrifice of self-interest. Egoism is outcome-for-me, duty ethics is rule-regardless-of-outcome. In engineering, duty ethics fits codes of ethics that bind the engineer to public safety; egoism fits badly, since a professional is trusted to put clients and the public above personal gain.

  • CAT 1 • Q2b • 6 marks; also 2017 Q2D • 6 marks
    Differentiate between the different available methods of intellectual property protection.
    Model Answer & Marker Words

    The government grants limited ownership rights to creators through four methods.

    1. Trade secrets. A trade secret is a confidential piece of IP that provides a company with a competitive advantage, for example the formula for Coca-Cola syrup or customer lists. The owner must take active measures to keep it secret, such as locked boxes, non-disclosure agreements and non-compete clauses. The Uniform Trade Secrets Act gives civil liability for misappropriation. It lasts only while the secret is kept.

    2. Trademarks. A trademark is a word, logo or sound that identifies a company or a product. The company can sue for improper use, but it can lose the mark if the name becomes a common noun, as with aspirin or escalator.

    3. Patents. A patent is a public document giving a detailed description of an invention. It gives the owner the exclusive right to prevent others from making, using or selling the invention for 20 years.

    4. Copyrights. Copyright protects an original work such as a book, song or program and gives the owner five rights: reproduction, distribution, public display, public performance and production of derivative works. The length is set by copyright law.

    Difference in short: trade secrets protect by secrecy, trademarks identify the source, patents protect inventions through a public document for 20 years, and copyright protects the expression of original works.

    What is the consequentialist justification for laws that give ownership and control of software to individuals or corporations (proprietary software)? What is the basic dilemma in giving individuals the ownership and control of software they wrote?

    Consequentialist justification (3 marks). A consequentialist judges a law by its consequences for society as a whole. The justification is a chain of consequences:

    • If copying is allowed, software purchases fall, so developers earn less income.
    • With less income and no incentive, there is lower production of new software.
    • This means fewer benefits to society. So laws giving ownership and control to the creator (copyright, patents, trade secrets) lead to the greatest good: they encourage innovation and investment, and so more and better software for everyone.

    • rewards the programmer's labour and protects their right to own what they made (the rights-based side);
    • but it restricts access and can create monopoly power and high prices, even though software is easy to copy at almost no cost and copying does not take anything from the owner.

    So the dilemma is how to balance proprietary rights with public access and affordability: too little protection kills the incentive to create, too much blocks the public from benefiting and from building on earlier work.

  • Set 6 slide question • marks as set
    "Safety in a commodity comes with a price." Explain.
    Model Answer & Marker Words

    Absolute safety is never possible to attain, and safety can be improved in a product only with an increase in cost. The primary (production) cost P is therefore high for a highly safe, low-risk product.

    On the other hand, unsafe products incur secondary costs S to the producer: warranty costs, loss of goodwill, loss of customers, litigation and downtime costs. S is high for a highly risky product.

    The total cost T = P + S is U-shaped, with a minimum total cost point M below which cost cannot be reduced. If the risk at M is not acceptable, the producer must choose a lower acceptable risk, and the total cost will be higher than M. That extra cost is the price of safety.

Kantianism: Categorical Imperative & Universalizability Principle

"Kant's idea of universalization does not work in practice." Discuss. Explain Kant's criteria for a valid moral rule, and evaluate how relevant the universalizability principle is today with practical examples.
Tested Papers & Exam Variants with Model Answers (2)
  • QB E4 • 6 marks • 2018 Morality c
    "Kant's idea of universalization does not work in practice." Discuss.
    Model Answer & Marker Words

    Kant's idea. A rule is valid only if it can be universally applied to everyone and it treats people as ends, not means. We test a rule by asking what would happen if everyone followed it. Example: the rule "I may make promises intending to break them" is universalised as "everyone may make and break promises". That makes promises unbelievable, which contradicts the wish to be believed, so the rule fails.

    Why it works. It is rational, produces universal moral guidelines and treats all persons as moral equals. It rejects "whatever it takes" reasoning and valid rules such as do not kill, lie or steal come out right.

    Why it fails in practice.

    • It allows no exceptions to moral rules. "Do not kill" gives no answer on a battlefield.
    • Sometimes no rule adequately characterizes an action, so the test cannot be run. The same act can be described by several rules.
    • There is no way to resolve a conflict between rules. "Do not lie" and "protect life" collide when telling the truth endangers someone.
    • It disregards consequences: "do not lie" even when not lying will lead to bad consequences.

    Conclusion. Universalisation is a strong test for clear cases such as promise-breaking, but real situations involve exceptions and competing duties. It works as a guide, not as a complete decision procedure, so the statement is partly true.

  • QB C1 • 8 marks
    Madge's shop has been robbed. She says to Harold, a police officer: "If you find the thief, I will pay you $500." Harold, through the course of his police investigations, finds the thief, and the thief is convicted of the robbery. Madge refuses to pay. Advise Harold. [reconstructed from garbled scan]
    Model Answer & Marker Words

    Issue. Is Madge's promise a legally enforceable contract, so that Harold can make her pay the $500?

    Rule. A contract is a mutual agreement between parties to a transaction that provides benefits to each. It needs mutual consent, offer and acceptance and consideration. It need not be in writing, so the spoken promise is not a problem by itself.

    Application.

    1. Offer. Madge made a definite promise: $500 for finding the thief. It was addressed to Harold, and it is clear and specific. beyond the slides In common law such a reward is a unilateral offer, accepted by doing the act (the Carbolic Smoke Ball case). Harold does not need to say "I accept". Finding the thief is the acceptance.
    2. Mutual consent. Both sides understood the terms. Nothing suggests force or misleading.
    3. Consideration. This is the crux. Consideration needs each party to receive some benefit it did not have before. Madge gains the thief's capture. But Harold is a police officer, and finding thieves is already his public duty. He gave Madge nothing he was not already bound to give. beyond the slides The common-law rule is that performing a duty you already owe, here a duty to the public, is not good consideration for a new promise. English cases apply this: England v Davidson (1840), where a police officer could not claim a reward for doing his duty, and Collins v Godefroy (1831), where a witness already bound to attend court could not claim payment for attending.
    4. Exception. beyond the slides The rule changes if Harold did something clearly beyond his normal duty, such as working in his own time or doing extra work Madge asked for. The facts say he found the thief "through the course of his police investigations", so he did only his job.

    Conclusion on the law. Probably no enforceable contract, so Harold would most likely lose in court. I would advise him that a claim is weak, and that the outcome depends on whether any part of his work went beyond his police duty.

    Ethical advice. The law is not the end of the matter. Madge made a promise and Harold relied on it. She benefited, and refusing to pay is dishonest and unfair, because she gets what she asked for and keeps the money. Her conduct fails the spirit of the deal even if it passes the letter of contract law. Ethically she ought to pay. On Harold's side, a police officer may be barred by service rules from taking rewards for official work. beyond the slides The slides' conflict-of-interest rule says engineers must not accept considerations from other parties while doing work for their employers (section t09). Harold should check whether he may accept the money at all, and should report it to his superiors. Being unable to sue does not make Madge's refusal right.

    Summary. Offer yes, consent yes, consideration no, because police duty already existed. No legal remedy likely, but a clear moral duty on Madge to pay.

    The ethical conundrum is the need to balance the needs of those who use information against the privacy rights of the people whose information is used.   • QB A2 • 8 marks
    What are the main ethical issues in sustaining a delicate balance between Privacy and Security issues when monitoring/censoring use of the internet technologies? Discuss with examples.
    Model Answer & Marker Words

    Governments and organisations monitor and censor Internet use to keep people secure (fighting terrorism, crime, fraud, harmful content). But monitoring intrudes on the right to privacy. The ethical issue is how to balance the two, so reasonable limits are set.

    1. Loss of control over personal information. Monitoring strips people of the power to control their own information. Example: under the Patriot Act, a pen register or trap and trace device can log every number a person calls or is called from, without a court order.

    2. Abuse of power and loss of checks and balances. Surveillance without a court order or independent oversight can be misused. Example: the USA Patriot Act 2001 allowed wiretaps without court order, and critics said it removed checks and balances. Powers can be used for intimidation of political dissenters or blackmail.

    3. Freedom of speech and expression. Censorship limits free speech (First Amendment idea) and access to information. People who know they are watched stop speaking freely. Example: a state blocking social media during protests or elections.

    4. Anonymity and confidentiality. Monitoring defeats anonymity and breaks encryption. Example: Moxie Marlinspike was asked to help a telecom intercept encrypted WhatsApp and Twitter traffic, which would expose every user, not only terrorists.

    5. Proportionality and consent. Is the intrusion in proportion to the threat, and do people know they are being watched? Transparency and consent reduce the harm. Example: employers who monitor email must tell staff of the policy.

    6. Security of the collected data. Stored data can be leaked or stolen. Example: a leaked government database can expose millions to identity theft.

    Conclusion. Security is a legitimate aim, but surveillance and censorship must be limited, lawful, transparent and under independent oversight, so that technology protects privacy rather than diminishes it.

    Outline the history of copyright law in the United States and give three criticisms of making the protection period longer.
    Model Answer & Marker Words

    History (3 marks). The first copyright law was passed in 1790 and gave 28 years (14+14) of protection for books. Each subsequent revision broadened coverage and increased the protection period: the 1831 Act added prints and sheet music (42 years), the 1909 Act added photos, recordings and movies (56 years), the 1976 Act added software and databases (75 years), and the 1998 Act added music broadcast over the Internet, with protection for the author's lifetime plus 95 years.

    Criticisms (3 marks). The Constitution says copyright is meant to promote the progress of science and useful arts by securing exclusive rights for limited times. Longer protection works against this because:

    • it benefits only the few owners of franchises, not creators or the public;
    • the 1998 Act is nicknamed the "Mickey Mouse Protection Act", showing it served big corporate owners;
    • it will shift the progress of science and arts offshore, to countries where the copyrights would have expired.
  • Practice • 6 marks
    Explain why the Digital Millennium Copyright Act is controversial. Give three consequences.
    Model Answer & Marker Words

    The DMCA was passed in 1998 and is still controversial because it makes circumventing copy control a felony, even if it is for a fair-use purpose. It therefore undermines fair use, and it makes many copyright violations felonies. Consequences:

    • Inconsistent rules: making a personal copy of a music CD is fair use, but doing the same for a movie DVD is illegal because the DVD's encryption would have to be circumvented.
    • It is illegal to play a DVD on a GNU/Linux machine, because the decryption program needed counts as circumvention.
    • Online service providers may be held liable, and face severe penalties, for copyright violations by their users. The Viacom v. YouTube lawsuit tests this.

  • 2016 Q4C • 6 marks; also exam 2021-22 solution file Q5d
    In a few sentences, explain why software poses a challenge to these traditional forms of intellectual ownership.
    Model Answer & Marker Words

    Traditional forms of ownership (copyright, patents and trade secrets) were designed for physical or printed things. Software challenges them because:

    • Digital copies are easy to make and perfect: software can be copied at almost no cost, so the owner cannot control reproduction and distribution. Copying also does not remove anything from the owner, unlike stealing a physical object.
    • The Internet and peer-to-peer networks let strangers share files globally and instantly, outside traditional distribution channels, which makes enforcement across borders very hard.
    • Software does not fit neatly into one form of protection. Copyright protects only the expression of an idea, not the idea, and the object code, not the source code. Patents are hard to apply because it is hard to decide what is "obvious", so some bad patents have been issued. Critics say traditional copyright has provided sufficient protection and patents are costly and cover trivial inventions.
    • Open-source and free software reject ownership altogether and share source code freely (see t15).

    The result is a conflict between rewarding creators and giving the public access, so the law keeps being stretched (longer terms, DMCA) and remains controversial.
